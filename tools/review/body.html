Corrections from the verification pass

Three things the logs said and I had not

Yield

Logs/071 defines yield as the fraction of material that survives a step — a mass fraction, not a probability of success. And the analysis code reinterprets it as a coin flip (X_b ~ Bern(q)), which is what the old error bars were drawing. The appendix figure now shows mean route yield, which needs no such assumption.

“One plate”

Removed. A plate runs one reaction across many reagents; the children of a hub are generally made by different reactions. Logs/073 does measure genuine parallel arrays — but calls itself a curiosity, not a publication claim, and is unreplicated across generators, so it is not substituted in.

A stale number

Our cost model was quoted as “0.0–3.1% off optimal”. That was measured on the naive child policy. Re-audited against the headline configuration it is __GAP__%, and it now appears as Table 1's verification line rather than as a row.

A naming collision, now fixed “best-molecule order” used to sit at 70 modes here while Figure 1's “best-candidate” sits at ~29, and the two names invited exactly the wrong read. They are different things: hub order: best child score is hub-batching with hubs ranked by their best child's score, while best-candidate is the baseline strategy that never enumerates hub children. All four orderings beat the baseline — even the deliberately reversed one, 42 against 29.
Two honesty fixes The colour encoding is gone: it coloured by “reads a signal”, but reverse-flow does read the flow — it inverts it. And __NPL__ cells are pool-limited, where the comparison degenerates and the greedy can even come out faster. Excluding them is both more honest and stronger: the wall-clock floor rises from 0.6× to 5.6×. Say “up to 80×”, not “1/80th”.
Training time is excluded, deliberately Training is ~50–100 GPU-h per cell against 2,481–7,631 s of enumeration, and all four arms read one shared checkpoint. Adding it applies a constant 24–145× larger than the whole between-arm spread, flattening the panel. Training cancels from every comparison in the paper — a point in the method's favour, and a sentence rather than an axis.

Against someone else’s pipeline

S3-GFN generates, a retrosynthesis planner routes its molecules over its own catalogue, and SPARROW — another group’s optimizer — chooses what to make. Three rows, one seed, one gate.

The margin moved — lead with __LEAD__× The competitor is now budget-matched and properly sampled (438 of 500 molecules routed, 181 distinct modes available), and it is a far stronger opponent than the one we were quoting. On seed 43 at gate 7.0, both solves optimal, SPARROW-selects went 24 → 46 modes (3.42× → 1.76×) and greedy selection 38 → 55 (2.16× → 1.47×). The old numbers came from a checkpoint trained far longer than the generators it was compared against: superseded, not merely older. Both sides are now the same seed, which the previous version could not manage.
Exact, not bounded The greedy arm's 5-mode grid could only place R=100 between 55 and 59, so it was re-priced at 1-mode spacing: 55 modes fit in 99 reactions, 56 need 102. Nine solves, all optimal, minutes of CPU — and it removes an interval from the headline denominator.
Naive pool only A diversity-enforced (“pruned”) pool — 500 mutually different molecules rather than the 500 highest-scoring — exists for this seed and gives a different picture. It is under review rather than quoted, and the table carries a line saying so. Which pool is the fair comparison is methodological, not cosmetic: pruning changes what the competitor optimises over.
Selection-only control, on DRD2 Handing SPARROW our own enumerated children isolates the chooser from the candidates. Pool verified byte-identical by digest on both seeds — the check Logs/062 lacked: it recovers 28 and 15 distinct molecules against our 78, both solves optimal. A lower bound, because our arm sees only stocked fragments while SPARROW sees the whole enumeration.
Verification, a sentence not a row We independently priced hub-batching’s own selection with SPARROW and the two agree to __GAP__% on __CFG__. Count-once charges what we actually spent; SPARROW charges only what hindsight needed, so our published cost is conservative.

Figure 3 · selection vs enumeration

Buying compounds is not buying distinct molecules

The sharpest objection to this work is your generator made the molecules, so why do we need your selection? Here SPARROW — given a diversity term its authors say it does not ship — chooses from an enumerated pool under the same budget we spend, at reward > 7.0 and Tanimoto < 0.5. It buys nearly a hundred compounds either way. The grey is what those compounds amount to once near-duplicates collapse; our bar has none, because the greedy cannot accept a duplicate.

The third panel shows why, without appealing to the collapse rule at all. It is the distribution of pairwise Tanimoto within each arm’s own selection — how alike the compounds an arm bought are to each other. Ours sits low and tight; both SPARROW arms sit more than twice as high. The duplicate counting in the left panel is a consequence of that distribution, not an artefact of where we put the threshold.

Appendix A1 · promoted from limitations

How much material survives a route

A reaction count treats every reaction as equal. Each has a yield — the fraction of material surviving the step — and compounding those along a route gives the share reaching the final compound.

The mechanism is unambiguous. Mean step yield is __STEPLO__–__STEPHI__ for both arms on every cell, so hub-batching is not choosing better chemistry. What differs is route length: its routes are shorter, so less yield compounds away.

What this deliberately no longer shows An earlier version drew error bars from a batch-failure model that treats each route yield as a coin-flip success probability. That is an assumption, not a measurement, and an error bar lends it precision it has not earned. The structural point behind it is real and belongs in prose: molecules built off one shared intermediate share its route prefix, so their outcomes are correlated in a way independent molecules’ are not. The numbers stay in the CSV, unplotted, with the assumption named.

Landed since these figures were built

Assessed, not yet used

Four things finished after the exhibits above were harvested. None are in a figure yet; two of them change what a figure can honestly say.

  • S3-GFN now has both selection arms on one retrosynthesis, nine cells. Greedy and SPARROW read off the identical planned routes, and every cell reports pool size, makeable, delivered and reactions used, so a small pool cannot masquerade as a large one (Logs/075, s3gfn_selection_R100.csv). This is the open Table 1 seed question, answered — but on enlarged pools costing 10,271 / 41,346 / 10,342 extra scoring calls, up to four times the model’s own training budget. That number has to travel with any row taken from them, and the seeds differ fourfold in how hard they were to enlarge, so per-seed, not averaged.
  • On the enlarged sEH pool the standard pipeline beats us, 100 distinct to 82 — and the reason is the interesting part. 98 of its 100 selected molecules are one reaction from purchasable material; all 144 starting materials are genuinely in the catalogue. One step per molecule is the cheapest anything can be, so the solver was handed a shopping list rather than out-planning anyone. This is a property of the generator: one-step rates are S3-GFN 39–80%, REINVENT 9–34%, Saturn 0–16%. The defensible claim is not “we beat the standard pipeline” but “we beat it where synthesis is non-trivial” — and Saturn is already routed on seven cells, so that test needs no new generation. Reporting the loss with its cause is a stronger position than a table where it never appears.
  • FragGFN was training on 32× the competitor budget; all nine cells are retrained. The two halves of the benchmark normalize on different quantities — the competitor block on scoring calls (~10,000, each program’s own default), the matrix on training steps (5,000) — and a training step scores a whole batch. FragGFN belongs with S3-GFN, not RGFN, since it has no reaction model (Logs/076). Six of nine cells are ready; the three DRD2 cells hold 376–435 diverse molecules against a target of 500. The other three matrix generators are still on the training-step convention, and whether that is a real unfairness has not been measured.
  • Every gate is now the 5%-FPR point on its own actives-vs-decoys set: sEH 5.68, DRD2 0.345, ClpP −9.1, 6TD3-B 6.718. The figures above still gate sEH at 5.0 (Fig 1) and 7.0 (Fig 3 / Table 1). One loose end worth closing before anything is quoted: targets.py carries 6.718 for 6TD3-B while a docstring in calibrate_gates.py still names 7.97.

Open

What still needs a decision

  • Seeds 42 and 44 for Table 1 — now solved, but on enlarged pools. The table above is still n=1 because the harvest predates them. The solves exist (see the section before this), and they carry a sampling asymmetry that has to be disclosed in the same sentence as the numbers. This matters more at 1.47× than it did at 3.42×: seed variance could move the margin materially.
  • Which competitor pool is the fair one — highest-scoring, or diversity-enforced? The pruned variant exists for seed 43 and answers differently. Logs/067 exists because this is genuinely not obvious.
  • The sEH selection-only control does not converge, and has stopped. Two attempts: the full 50k pool stalled, and a further pass ran ~12 h per seed and still returned TimeLimit. The withdrawn 2.27× cannot be recovered as a ratio on sEH — which is why the DRD2 control is the form the claim now takes.
